Voyager 1 Is Now One Light-Day From Earth

After more than 49 years in flight, NASA's farthest spacecraft has crossed a distance light needs a full day to travel.

NASA's Voyager 1 is now one light-day from Earth, the agency announced Nov. 19. The milestone came at 2:16 a.m. Pacific time on Nov. 18, according to NASA, more than 49 years after the probe launched in 1977.

What a light-day means

A light-day is the distance light covers in 24 hours. For Voyager 1, NASA put that at about 16.1 billion miles, or 25.9 billion kilometers. In practical terms, a radio command sent from Earth now takes a full day to reach the spacecraft, and any reply takes another day to come back.

NASA said mission engineers confirmed the milestone using the probe's carrier signal along with trajectory calculations. The carrier signal is the steady radio tone the spacecraft sends home, even when it is not transmitting data.

A problem in the background

The milestone arrived during a technical hiccup. NASA said the team has received no science or engineering data from Voyager 1 since Nov. 14, and engineers are still analyzing the cause. The carrier signal is still reaching Earth, according to the agency, which means the spacecraft is still transmitting.

Who is behind it

Voyager is run by NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory in Southern California, which is managed by Caltech, and the probes talk to Earth through NASA's Deep Space Network of large radio dishes. NASA's release included remarks from Joe Westlake, director of the agency's Heliophysics Division, JPL director Dave Gallagher, Voyager project scientist Linda Spilker and project manager Suzanne Dodd. Caltech president Ray Jayawardhana used the moment to honor Ed Stone, who guided the mission for decades.

Why it matters

Voyager 1 is the most distant object people have built. It crossed into interstellar space in 2012, NASA said, and it and its twin, Voyager 2, still take measurements of how the Sun's magnetic influence changes at great distances. Data from that far out cannot be gathered any other way.

What comes next

Engineers will keep working on the data problem, sending commands on a weekly schedule. NASA said both Voyagers are expected to keep science instruments running until at least their 50th launch anniversaries next summer, helped by an earlier change that saves power on the aging probes.
