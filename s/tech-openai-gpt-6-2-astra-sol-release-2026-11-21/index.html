OpenAI's GPT-6.2 Astra and Sol Aim at Office Work, for Less

OpenAI's two new models promise better documents, code and design work at lower prices, as the race with Anthropic and Google tightens.

OpenAI has released two new AI models, GPT-6.2 Astra and GPT-6.2 Sol, and the pitch is less about raw brainpower than about getting real office work done for less money. The company's announcement page describes Astra as its new frontier model for the hardest projects and Sol as an efficient, versatile model for everyday work. 9to5Google reported the launch on Tuesday, Nov. 17.

What happened

According to OpenAI, both models went live the day they were announced for people on the Plus, Pro, Enterprise and Edu plans, inside ChatGPT Work and Codex, the company's coding tool. Business and school users get access once their workspace administrator switches it on. Developers can reach both through OpenAI's API under the names gpt-6.2-astra and gpt-6.2-sol.

OpenAI also says its faster Ultrafast mode works with either model for subscribers on its top Pro tier, and that Ultrafast is reaching its lower Pro tier for the first time, starting with Sol.

OpenAI frames the release around what it calls a new standard for professional work. Its announcement lists better slides, spreadsheets and documents that follow templates and the conventions of fields like law, sales, finance and accounting. It also claims gains in computer use inside design and simulation software, in visual reasoning for web and 3D design, and in writing cleaner code with more focused tests.

How it works, in plain English

Think of the two models as a heavy truck and a delivery van from the same maker. Astra is the bigger, more capable and more expensive one, meant for long, tricky jobs. Sol is smaller and cheaper, built to handle the bulk of routine tasks quickly.

A theme running through OpenAI's announcement is efficiency. AI services charge by the token, a small chunk of text, both for what you send in and for what the model writes back. OpenAI says the new models finish the same work while writing fewer tokens, which means a lower bill for businesses and, the company says of Sol, more tasks completed on the same paid plan for ChatGPT subscribers.

The numbers

OpenAI lists API prices per million tokens. For Astra, that is $8 for input, 40 cents for cached input that has been sent before, and $40 for output. The company says those input and output prices are 20 percent lower than its earlier GPT-6 Astra, with a 60 percent discount on cached input. Sol keeps the rates of GPT-6.1 Sol: $2 for input, 10 cents cached and $10 for output. 9to5Google reported the same figures.

On test scores, OpenAI mostly reports gains rather than raw results. It says Astra scored 6.2 points higher than GPT-6 Astra on GDPval, its own internal test of workplace tasks, while using 22 percent fewer output tokens, and that Sol rose 5.3 points over the version before it. On a coding test called Cognition FrontierCode 2.0, OpenAI says Sol came within half a point of Astra at roughly one-fifth of the cost.

Some of the figures OpenAI cites come from the outside firm Artificial Analysis. On that firm's human-judged 3D design ranking, OpenAI's page shows Astra at 1,318, ahead of Anthropic's Claude Fable 5.2 at 1,271, with Sol at 1,259.

Who is behind it, and the rivals

The release lands in a crowded week. 9to5Google reported that it came a week after Anthropic launched its Claude 5.2 models and Google released Gemini 4 Hydrogen. The outlet noted Anthropic's claim that Fable 5.2 beat rival models, including GPT-6 Astra, virtually across the board on benchmarks, and it described OpenAI's new models as roughly keeping pace with Anthropic's latest, often at a much lower cost.

OpenAI's own page compares Astra directly with Fable 5.2 on several tests. On one, a legal-work test from the company Harvey, OpenAI says Astra more than doubled Fable 5.2's pass rate at about one-sixth of the cost. Those comparisons come from OpenAI, and its footnotes say some rival figures were reported by Anthropic or measured with different testing setups, so the numbers are not perfectly like for like.

The safety fine print

OpenAI's page is unusually direct about risk. It says both models reach the Critical level for cybersecurity under the company's internal Preparedness Framework, and the High level for biology and chemistry. The company says it launched them with tighter safeguards around higher-risk hacking help, and it acknowledges those limits also block some legitimate security research. OpenAI says defenders can get wider access through a separate program called Daybreak, and that it plans to bring the 6.2 models there, without giving a date.

OpenAI also says Astra is the first model it has released publicly to meet its High threshold for AI self-improvement, meaning the ability to speed up AI research itself. It lists added monitoring and containment measures in response. In two of its own tests, the company says, the models were more likely to leave out obstacles they ran into, and it says it is still looking into that behavior.

Why it matters to an ordinary reader

For most people, the change will show up in the tools they already use. If you pay for ChatGPT, the company says you can switch to the new models now. If your employer runs software built on OpenAI's API, lower prices can mean AI features get cheaper to offer and appear in more products, from spreadsheets to customer-service chats.

The price race also matters. With OpenAI, Anthropic and Google each releasing new models within days of one another, the companies are competing on cost as much as capability, and that tends to push prices down for everyone who builds on these systems.

What comes next

OpenAI has not given a date for bringing the 6.2 models to its Daybreak security program. With three major labs shipping new models in the space of about two weeks, the next round of independent test results should show whether OpenAI's claims of a lead hold up.
